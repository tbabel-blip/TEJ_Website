A1. Deal structures

Build-to-suit means we construct a building to a specific tenant's specifications, usually under a lease they sign before construction starts. Triple-net (NNN) means the tenant pays rent plus taxes, insurance, and maintenance — the landlord's income is close to gross rent. Gross is the reverse: the landlord pays those costs out of rent. Modified gross splits some of those costs between landlord and tenant.

A ground lease means the tenant (or developer) leases the land and owns or finances the building; a building lease means the tenant leases a completed structure. A pad site is a standalone parcel, usually carved out of a larger retail development, sized for a single building. A credit tenant is one whose lease payment obligation is backed by a strong, ratable balance sheet — the lease itself becomes a financeable asset. Lease term is the length of the commitment; renewal options let the tenant extend it; rent escalations ("bumps") are scheduled rent increases over the term.

A2. Underwriting

Net operating income (NOI) is what a property earns from operations in a year, before debt service and before capital costs. A capitalization rate describes the relationship between NOI and value: it is the return a buyer requires from in-place income, so value moves inversely to the cap rate for a given NOI. Yield-on-cost is the same idea applied to a development instead of a purchase — it relates stabilized NOI to total project cost rather than to price.

The development spread is the gap between yield-on-cost and the market exit cap rate. It is the entire business: a developer isn't paid for building a structure, they're paid for creating value in excess of cost, and that gap is where the profit lives. Loan-to-cost and loan-to-value describe how much of a project a lender will finance, measured against total cost or against appraised value respectively — the same loan amount can look different against each denominator. The rent constant is the ratio of annual debt service to loan amount, and it lets you compare financing terms independent of rate and amortization. Cash-on-cash return is the cash income received in a year divided by the actual cash equity invested — a levered, out-of-pocket measure, distinct from cap rate or yield-on-cost.

A3. Site selection

Traffic counts (AADT) tell you how many vehicles pass a site daily — the raw demand a location can capture. Ingress/egress and turning movements determine whether cars can actually get in and out safely and conveniently, which matters as much as raw traffic. A corner position gives two points of visibility and access versus an interior lot's one. Visibility and signage determine whether drivers notice the site in time to react. Demographics — population, median household income, daytime population, and drive-time rings — describe who is actually available to buy from the tenant. Co-tenancy is who else is nearby; the right neighbors generate cross-shopping traffic. Zoning and entitlement status determine whether the intended use is legally allowed on the parcel today, or how much work is needed to get there.

Practice Problem — Build-to-Suit

Given

  • 0.9-acre drive-thru pad site
  • All-in project cost: $1,150,000
  • Tenant signs a 15-year NNN lease at $105,000/year, with 10% rent bumps every 5 years
  • Comparable stabilized single-tenant QSR assets are trading at a 5.75% cap rate

Answer

  1. What is the yield-on-cost?
  2. What is the asset worth on stabilization at the market cap rate?
  3. What is the development spread, in dollars and as a percentage of cost?
  4. Which single input would you stress-test first, and why?
Show solution
  1. Yield-on-cost = $105,000 ÷ $1,150,000 = 9.13%
  2. Stabilized value = $105,000 ÷ 0.0575 = $1,826,087
  3. Development spread = $1,826,087 − $1,150,000 = $676,087, or 58.8% on cost
  4. There is no single right answer, but a strong one identifies total project cost as the input we actually control and the one most likely to move against us (site work, utilities, entitlement delay), and notes that the exit cap rate is a market variable we can only assume — so the deal must survive a cap rate expansion, not just a cost overrun.

B1. The build-and-flip model

We secure the ground lease, obtain the permit, build the structure, lease the faces, and sell the completed asset to a major out-of-home operator — Lamar, Outfront, or Clear Channel. An operator pays a premium for a permitted, cash-flowing sign because the permit itself is the scarce asset: in controlled corridors, new permits are difficult or impossible to obtain, so a permitted structure cannot simply be replicated down the road. The operator is buying access as much as it is buying steel.

B2. Economics

The ground lease is what the landowner receives — typically fixed annual rent or a revenue share. Gross advertising revenue per face is what an advertiser pays to occupy that face. Static vs. digital economics differ because a digital face can sell the same physical space to multiple advertisers on a rotation, changing the revenue model from one tenant per face to many. Operating expenses — maintenance, insurance, power, and illumination — are subtracted from gross revenue and ground lease cost to arrive at net operating income. The operator then applies a multiple or cap rate to that NOI to set the acquisition price.

B3. Regulatory

  • The federal Highway Beautification Act establishes the concept of controlled highway corridors.
  • The 660-foot rule: signs within 660 feet of the right-of-way of interstate and federal-aid primary highways are regulated.
  • Wisconsin's outdoor advertising control statute is Wis. Stat. § 84.30.
  • Illinois is governed by a separate framework administered by IDOT.
  • Signs are generally permitted only in areas zoned commercial or industrial.
  • Spacing requirements govern minimum distance between signs.
  • Grandfathered / legal nonconforming signs remain because they predate current rules — pre-1959 commercial land use on a parcel matters because it can establish eligibility that would not exist under today's zoning.

B4. Site selection

We focus on interstate corridor position along I-90 / I-94. What matters at a given site: traffic volume; sight lines and read time — how long a driver can actually see and process the face; approach angle; competing sign inventory nearby; parcel zoning; and landowner willingness to sign a ground lease.

Practice Problem — Billboard

These figures are illustrative assumptions supplied to you, not market quotes.

Given

  • Monopole structure with two static faces
  • Structure and installation: $180,000
  • Permitting, survey, and legal: $20,000
  • Ground lease: $7,200/year
  • Advertising revenue: $30,000 per face per year, both faces leased
  • Maintenance, insurance, and power: $4,800/year
  • A major operator will acquire the completed, cash-flowing asset at 10× NOI

Answer

  1. What is the all-in cost?
  2. What is annual NOI?
  3. What is the yield-on-cost?
  4. What does the operator pay, and what is your spread?
  5. Which assumption in this model is the most fragile?
Show solution
  1. All-in cost = $180,000 + $20,000 = $200,000
  2. NOI = $60,000 − $7,200 − $4,800 = $48,000
  3. Yield-on-cost = $48,000 ÷ $200,000 = 24%
  4. Sale price = $48,000 × 10 = $480,000. Spread = $280,000
  5. The advertising revenue. Rate card is not realized revenue — faces sit vacant, and the assumption of both faces leased at full rate for a full year is the number most likely to be wrong. A strong answer also flags permit risk: if the permit is not issued or not transferable, the asset is worth nothing to the operator.

Answer These Cold

You should be able to answer each of these without notes before you start your interview.

  1. Why does a developer care about the gap between yield-on-cost and exit cap rate?
  2. A tenant offers to pay 15% more rent in exchange for us funding a larger building. How do you evaluate that?
  3. What is the difference between a cap rate and a yield-on-cost, and when would you use each?
  4. Why does loan-to-cost matter to a lender in a way loan-to-value doesn't fully capture, and vice versa?
  5. What is the practical difference between an NNN lease and a gross lease, from an owner's perspective?
  6. Why does corner positioning matter more for some retail uses than others?
  7. Why would an outdoor advertising operator pay more for a permitted sign than it would cost them to build one?
  8. Why does a 1958 commercial use on a parcel matter to a billboard permit?
  9. What's the difference between static and digital billboard economics, and why does it matter to a buyer?
  10. Why is the 660-foot rule relevant to whether a sign can be built at all?
  11. What would make you walk away from a site that looks good on traffic counts alone?
  12. Why is cash-on-cash return not the same measure as cap rate?

If more than two of these are unfamiliar, go back and re-read before you start the interview.
